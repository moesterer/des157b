Annotated Bibliography

Dominus, Susan. “Longevity Science Is Overhyped. But This Research Really Could Change Humanity.” The New York Times Magazine, 27 Apr. 2026, updated 1 May 2026.

The article examines recent breakthroughs in longevity science, but specifically cellular rejuvenation and partial epigenetic reprogramming, while also questioning the hype surrounding reverse aging. It is useful because it goes into the scientific specifics while building a rough timeline of breakthroughs and how they led to others. It separates the real medical possibilities of rejuvenation science from the exaggerated promises often made by the wellness industry, billionaires, and biotech companies. It also discusses both scientific marvel and hope while also acknowledging the darker parts including the role of wealthy investors like Silicon Valley billionaires and companies such as Altos Labs. Dominus is a staff writer for The New York Times Magazine and bases the article on interviews with scientists, biotech leaders, and researchers in the field. The article concludes that rejuvenation research could potentially change medicine by treating age-related diseases, but full-body age reversal is still uncertain and risky as we don’t know how interconnected systems (like the whole body rather than the isolated system of the eye) would react. This article supports the idea that even if reverse-aging technology begins as medical research, it could become a tool of inequality if only the wealthy and powerful can access it.

Guriev, Sergei, and Daniel Treisman. “How Do Dictatorships Survive in the 21st Century?” Carnegie Corporation of New York, 6 Dec. 2022.

This article explains how modern dictatorships survive (through technology, globalization, etc) not only through fear, violence, and repression, but through manipulation of public opinion, media, elections, and democratic language. My project imagines a future where powerful leaders use reverse-aging technology to stay in power longer, and this article helps explain how authoritarian leaders maintain control while still pretending to support democracy which gives possible context for how a dictatorship could come about in the west, especially by people who live as long as multiple lifetimes. The timeline of contrast between older dictators of fear (Stalin/Mao), and modern dictators who use media, consultants, public image, and misinformation to make their rule seem normal or popular (presenting themselves as agents of democracy and humble servants for the people) could help inform my narrative for how my imagined dictatorship has shaped an individual’s life. The author, Guriev is a professor of economics at Sciences Po, while Treisman is a UCLA political science professor and Andrew Carnegie Fellow focusing on threats to democracy. Modern autocrats often succeed by reshaping what citizens believe rather than relying only on obvious violence that is not as helpful anymore. This supports the idea that an immortal or very long-lived ruler would not need to openly announce tyranny. Rather, they could outlive most people and rewrite history, control public belief, and present their extended rule as stability or progress., beneficial for the people.

Desjardins, Lisa, and Leila Jackson. “How the New Class of Billionaires Solidified Outsized Political Influence.” PBS NewsHour, 1 Jan. 2026.

This is a transcript that examines the growing political influence of billionaires in the US, especially through campaign spending, super PACs, and direct access to political power. As my project imagines a dystopian future where wealthy people use reverse-aging technology to preserve their influence, this article shows that billionaires already have an outsized role in elections and government (supporting my imagined future with current examples).The concrete statistics claim that the 100 richest Americans spent over $1 billion on federal elections in 2024, compared to $46 million in 2004. PBS NewsHour is a major public news program, and the actual interview has Beth Reinhard, a Washington Post reporter who researched billionaire political spending. Billionaire influence is increasing in scale and openness, though public backlash can still limit its power. If the rich gained access to life-extending technology before everyone else, their existing political influence could become even more permanent and unequal (exacerbating existing issues) and they could continue to support those who do not oppose them. The political leader (if not a billionaire themselves) and the billionaires would have a symbiotic system where they both benefit as the leader makes laws to support billionaires and billionaires fund the leader so they can stay in power.
